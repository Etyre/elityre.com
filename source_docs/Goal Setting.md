### Directions, not goals

With a few exceptions shese are not "goals" per se. Most are in some way ongoing. They're not objectives that I check off and move on to the next thing, rather this is a list of things that I care about and that I want to be part of my life.

Some of them could be naturally reformulated as goals. Very obviously, "dating and partner-search project" could be recast as "have an amazing romantic partner who I like and admire (and who likes me)". "Get rich; move towards minimum viable retirement" could be "have enough money to retire".  "Learn a lot of history" could turn into "know a lot of history."

Why not express them as goals? 

Well, for one thing, I don't want wether I'm satisified with my life, this month, to hinge on whether I've acomplished some important-to-me goal; even a very important one like romantic parnership (which I've historically found extremeley difficult to statisfy).

### Process

Coming to these goals is an iterative drafting process. On my Considering and choosing [[My goals and priorities]], page I will litterally write and rewrite draft objective lists.

For each objective, I do two checks:

1. Is this specific enough that it will be clear whether or not I hit the objective?
2. Murphyjitsu



Then I do a check on the whole list: is all this this reasonable and feasible to do given the time and resources available?

### Psychocomputational benefits

It's a cognitively expensive operation that takes multiple hours and entails loading up ~all my priorities in short term memory. But that's the point. For me, actually going through the process of selecting these goals, not just the final result, is where a lot of the value lies. 

Knowing and weighing costs

In [Productivity through self-loyalty](https://mindingourway.com/productivity-through-self-loyalty/) Nate writes,

> This is the mindstate in which I attain high productivity: various parts of the mob of my mind occasionally need rest, recuperation, and procrastination. Parts of me ask for these things. When they do, I ask them how much they really need, how much they can get by with. Do I actually need to take four days off? Because I will, but it's expensive.
>
> Often, when a part of me really needs a break, and throws up its hands feeling overwhelmed, its initial demands are unrealistic—"two weeks with no responsibilities!" So then I ask it again, with the demeanor of George Bailey, what it really needs to get by. And that part of me quickly remembers that all of me is in this together, and that I'm trying to do some very difficult things, and that all parts of me are constrained by scarce resources. Then the part that protested searches for what it really needs, the bare minimum, and it usually answers something like "I can get the rest I need in fifteen minutes."

I often strugged to run this mental operation. If I ask myself, or some part of myself, "how much do I/you really need?", I typically have something like a *confused* response. It's not clear how to answer that question. Like, for any given thing that some part of me wants, it can basically always go without, for at least one more hour, or one more day. It's not like I'm running out of litteral physical gas in a litteral physical tank, and if I don't fill up the car will litterally, physically not be able to go.<sup>1</sup> Where's the line between what I "actually" "need" and what I merely want.

The key to usefully exeuting this query is a sub operation that that Nate describes, but glosses over as automatic: "And that part of me quickly remembers that all of me is in this together, and that I'm trying to do some very difficult things, and that all parts of me are constrained by scarce resources."

In order for some part of me to evaluate what it *really* needs, the costs and tradeoffs have to be cognitively available to that part. It has to be abe to see and feel what happens as a result of it's demands. 

This context is *not* cognitively available by default (for me, at least). When some part of me wants to  stay in bed for another hour instead of getting up and getting to work, the costs don't just *feel* distant and fake, it's not clear that they actually, for real, matter. If I stay in bed for one extra hour, but otherwise work hard, then...I'll achieve all my goals one hour later? If that's the trade, it seems like a tottally fine deal.

Doing this objective setting exercise every month makes the costs and tradeoffs cognitively available to me. 

I look at the list of all the threads that I want to be part of my life, and I distill them down into concerete objectives that I could actually do (instead of just far-mode appealing abstractions), and I try to fit them all into the time and resources that I have available. And I find that they don't all fit, and that I have to make the bitter choice of letting some of them go for this, but hoping to come back to them, and pick them up in the next month.

This exercise puts me in touch with the real scarcity of my time and resources, and the real oportunity cost that I'm missing out on. 

And that lights a fire under my ass to execute, to not waste any time. But more critically, when I do this exercise I'm suddenly able to do the cogitive operation of "how much do I really need a break", because the part of me that needs a break now knows what the price is. 

It brings the costs, as well as the beneftis into near-mode, where I can evaluate and compare them.

Namely, that if I don't work tonight, I'm less likely to hit my goals for this month. If I don't hit my goals for this month that means, first of all, that I lose by the standards of the game that I'm playing—partially I'm just motivated to complete my objectives that I set for myself out of a sense of integrity and self-actualization. 

And failing to hit my objectives matters to me for three reasons:

1. Because it means that I'll lose by the standards of the game that I'm playing.
2. Because these objectives are concretizizations of my priorities, and I care about those objectives to the extent that I care about the priorities.  I want to Cultivate Amazing Focus. I want that to be part of my life. And the way that grounds out, concertely, this month, is doing 20 intentionality blocks, or not.
3. Because failing to hit my objecetives pushes away all the other priorities, the things that I care about, which I put aside for this month-period, in the hopes of maybe coming back to them in the next month-period.



And, secondarily, if I don't hit my goals this month, then I'll have even less capacity next month. The threads that I care about, the things that I want to be part of my life, but which I painfully set aside for this month, in the hopes of coming back to them next month, probably not make it in next month either.

And seeing that price, the part can weigh whether or not taking a break tonight is worth the pirce. 

This pricing is downstream of a cognitively expensive operation. I need to take the time and the space to load of all of that at once, and to make the tradeoffs and prioritization decisions, to feel how valuable my time is

Then my day-to-day self can make decisions about which specific thing I want to do next, while feeling the cost of not doing any particular thing.





When I don't do this exercise, I waste a lot more time. 



### Conserving attention

But the flip side is also true, by doing this exexcise, I actually can put away goals and threads that are important to me, that I want to be part of my life, but which I'm just actually not going to get to this month. 

I actually do the computation to evaluate which are most important to me, 



If I don't do this exercise, by default, I'm trying to manage all of these goals and priorities. Not all the same time—because I can't actually hold them all in my mind at the same time in day to day life—but in the ad hoc manner of trying to make progress on whichever one(s) were last made salient to me, so that I was in touch with the desire for that one to be part of my life. 

I know that I want all of these things in my life, and so I try hold on to all of them at once.

This doesn't work very well. There are usually increasing retruns to attention. I can't realisically do everything at once. I have to pick.

And so go through a cognitively laborious process of laying it all out, and comparing against what I have the resources for, and actually choosing.

And in doing that operation, I actually do put aside the things that are pulling at my attention. It's th same princple whereby a functional GTD system takes on the load of your implicit commitments, and your mind let's go of them, because they know that they'll be held and attended to by the system. The combination of having decided what to focus on, and knowing that your other priorities are written down and packed away, and I will come back and re-evaluate them the next time I decide, means quiets the parts of me that are afarid that I'll neglect these priorities that are, in fact, important to me.

But if they do, I can honestly say to them "We can't do everything, and we chose aim for these objectives, in this month-period. We'll choose again at the next-month period."
