Chasing my reading

Any time I read a book, I'll start by making a list of some questions that I'm interested in. Even if I'm picking up a book, on a topic that I know almost nothing about, out of general exploratory interest, I'll still push myself to generate some questions to focus my reading and thinking.

Note taking

I always take notes as I read.

At the end of every chapter, always summarize the chapter in my notes. This forces me to consolidate my understanding of what I just read, to draw out some specific learnings, instead of reading a bunch of words that have little permanent impact on my thoughts.

The point of reading is to have new thoughts, and mainly I'm wanting to notice nascent proto-thouhgts as they occur to me and, in the process of writing them down or stating them out loud, develop them into fully formed ideas.

[Quote myself from strategies for intentionally inducing thought.]

Knowing facts is mostly not very leveraged. I get value from reading mainly by building models of domains in the world, such that one can orient and navigate in those domains. To do that, I want to be exposed to new ideas, and I want to work with those ideas, by thinking, writing, or in the case of content specifically about skills, practice <link..

Knowing lots of facts is not usually very relevant to that.

But there are some domains where I do want to accumulate and memorize a body of factual knowledge, primarily history and the hard "stamp collecting" sciences like psychology, chemistry. For those domains, I'll distill and compress facts, inline, into cards to put into spaced repetition, as I read.

Workflows

I have three basic workflows for reading books.

Reading on my laptop

I'll copy the full text of a chapter into Roam / LogSeq (each paragraph is it's own block), and take notes inline.

Usually, I'll listen to an audio version, as I follow along in the next. Sometimes I'll listen to a produced audiobook, but more often I'll use the speechify extension, which has the advantage of highlighting words to help keep pace.

Reading paper books or on an e ink device

I use my finger to to read: running it under the words that I'm reading, with my eyes following my finger. (I can read faster this way.) I use KOReader on my Kobo device, in large part because that gives the settings options

At the same time, I'll take notes verbally, by recording myself and making a transcript with otter. This is the main way that I record notes and thoughts: tapping ideas into touch-keyboard, and even writing with a stylus, is too slow. These notes are automatically imported into my roam , for later review and processing.

This is, I think, less efficient than reading on my laptop, but it gives me more flexibility. I don't want to be looking at my laptop constantly: reading a physical book or e ink device is more comfortable.

Listening to audiobooks

Listening to audio books is even less efficient than reading paper or e ink book. But it does give me the option to physically move my body as I consume book content. During rest days, I sometimes like to walk around the neighborhood while listening to and taking notes on history books.

These days, I almost always read books on my laptop. I prefer the form factor of books or e-ink e-readers, but reading on my laptop has advantages that are hard to replicate. I always write notes as a read, and typing is just faster than writing for me.

Almost always, I open what I'm reading on the left half of my screen and my notes on the right half.

Speed reading and speech to text

I usually read with speechify, either the speechify web app (for reading books) or the browser extension (for reading blog posts).

This allows me to read both visually and auditorily at the same time. The moving cursor alone, without the audio allows me to read faster than I could unassisted.

I read most material at between 3x and 3.50x, or between 600wpm and 700 wpm.

When I do read paper or e-ink books, I read with my finger, as described in Superreading for Success.

Writing as I read

For everything I read (a blog post, an article, a tweet thread, a book chapter), I force myself to write something, even if only a single sentence. That might be a summary of the key point, a question I have, an objection to the claim. This shifts reading from a (sometimes) passive activity to an active cognitive activity for me.

If a source is not worth that much effort, then it's not worth my time in the first place.

The point of reading is to have new thoughts. By typing out my thoughts as I read, I'm turning fleeting impressions and barely conscious thoughts into something clearly stated enough to take as object, and reflect on. That gives those impressions the opportunity to generate additional thoughts in response.

This helps me consolidate my understanding of the ideas that I'm trying to absorb, make new connections,

Regularly, I start jotting down some thoughts (sometimes only very tangentially related to what I'm reading), and this starts the flow of thinking and writing develops from a would-be-fleeting-impression into a coherent thesis or question-exploration. If there's no reason to keep it private, I'll often publish a slightly edited version on twitter and threadreader via Typefully, or publish it as a short blog post.

In particular, at the end of every book chapter, I push myself to compress and summarize the key ideas of the chapter, to help me consolidate my understanding.

Kinds of notes

Thoughts and reflections

Sometimes I'm reading primarily to spur new thoughts.

When I'm reading a blog post or a philosophical essay, a piece trying to make the case for a particular conclusion, mainly I'm wanting to reflect on the ideas, evaluate their truth, and extend them to other domains. The thing that I want is to have new thoughts and ideas, as a result of reading.

Paraphrases and compressions

Sometimes I'm reading primarily to absorb and internalize models or facts.

When I'm reading textbooks, or other expository resources conveying settled science, I'm mostly wanting to understand the material by paraphrasing it and compressing it into an organized structure of concepts that I can hold well in my mind. Depending on the specifics, I also want to distill those concepts into atomic units to put into spaced repetition to review. The thing that I want is to come away correctly understanding and remembering the content.

Sometimes I'm aiming to do both, or something in between. When I read scientific papers at the frontier of a field, I'm both parsing and the results of the particular experiment, as well generating followup questions and hypotheses , and absorbing and But for all of the above goals, I want to be actively thinking and working with the material, not just passively running my eyes over it, and letting what sticks stick.

In practice, there's not a clean distinction between persuasive and expository texts

Writing as I read

The value of reading is the new thoughts that it induces and the mental connections that it allows me to form.

  • Either, I'm reading in the hopes of promoting new ideas, new connections, new questions and confusions, related (even tangentially related) to the material that I'm reading.

  • Or I'm trying to absorb and internalize developed models about a domains

  • Or I'm looking to learn specific facts for scaffolding models and worldview.
